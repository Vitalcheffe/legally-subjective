\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper filed both halves of a pre-registered experiment on the
predictability of Supreme Court votes. The frozen corpus exists, is
hash-chained, and is public; the strongest statistical baseline---one
bit of revealed ideology per justice---set a vote-level bar of 63.7\%
that three families of structured challengers failed to clear; and the
sealed fifty were scored exactly once, by a zero-shot reader, a
retrieval reader, and nine per-justice personas (seven QLoRA adapters
and, by pre-inscription, two biographical-prompt-only seats). The
predictable part of a modern justice's vote, to the resolution that
the tested metadata can see, is the justice. The written record was
the remaining candidate, and the strongest individual-prospective
probe of it returned null: the persona did not improve prediction of
the Court's hardest cases.

What the persona did instead is now mapped. It moved individual votes
without moving outcomes; it made every simulated justice vote more
conservatively than the justice it imitates; the moving was done by
the biographical prompt, not the fine-tuned adapter; and the
pre-registered accounts of persona quality failed where they were
testable and were falsified where they were decisive. The adapter
absorbs the voice and withholds the vote. All code, frozen artifacts,
and sealed predictions are public, so the finding is not an appeal to
authority but an invitation: the design survives replication at
larger scale unchanged, and any future claim that personalization
improves judicial prediction now has a pre-registered null to beat.
Beyond ideology, at the scale and under the constraints tested here,
the written record keeps its counsel.
