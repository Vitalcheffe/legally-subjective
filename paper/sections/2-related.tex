\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Statistical forecasting of the Court.}
The modern line begins with the Supreme Court Forecasting Project:
\citet{ruger2004} built a conditional-choice model with input from
political science theory and, over a single term, predicted 75\% of the
Court's affirm/reverse outcomes---against 59.1\% for a panel of
legal experts. The lesson was bruising for doctrine: a small
regression with ideological covariates beat trained lawyers reading
the briefs. A decade later, \citet{katz2017} scaled the exercise with
a random forest over 628 features and nearly two centuries of
decisions (1816--2013), reporting 70.2\% accuracy at the case level
and 71.9\% at the individual vote level, and concluding that the
Court behaves in a way that is, in their words, remarkably consistent
with a machine-learning account. Both efforts, however, evaluate
\emph{in sample across history}: the models see several centuries ---
including eras with much higher unanimity rates---and predict votes
whose contemporaneous context is far removed from today's Court.

\paragraph{The attitudinal model.}
Political science supplies the theoretical prior for why such models
work. \citet{segal2002} argue that justices decide largely as
attitudes wrapped in legal reasoning: revealed ideology dominates
doctrine, and case facts matter mostly as triggers for those
attitudes. Methodologically, \citet{martin2002} estimate dynamic
ideal points for every justice since 1953, giving the field a
continuous measure of the very covariate that our strongest baseline
--- per-justice modal direction---approximates with a single bit per
justice. Our results in Section~\ref{sec:protocol} are, in effect, a
modern, pre-registered replication of the attitudinal prior on a
nine-term window: the ideology bit is the signal; the structured case
context is not.

\paragraph{Authorship and style.}
The persona condition is a stylometry question in modern dress.
\citet{mosteller1964} settled the authorship of the disputed
\emph{Federalist} papers with function-word frequencies; the field
they founded has since shown that individual writing style is
persistent, measurable, and hard to disguise~\citep{juola2006}. If a
justice's voice is a stable individual signature, fine-tuning a
language model on their opinions should transfer to their future
opinions; the open question---the one this experiment isolates ---
is whether that stylistic footprint carries \emph{decision-relevant}
information beyond the ideology bit, or merely rhetorical habits.

\paragraph{Language models as forecasters of the Court.}
A newer line replaces the regression with a prompt. \citet{stillwell2026}
evaluate four frontier models as forecasters of Supreme Court
decisions and conclude that much of the models' justice-level
accuracy reflects the Court's ordinary ideological alignment rather
than case-specific legal analysis---a conclusion our sealed exam
reaches from the opposite direction, by fixing the ideological prior
as the comparator: at the vote level the per-justice prior dominates
every language condition (75.9\% against 50--60\%), and at the case
level the persona adds nothing to the zero-shot. \citet{posner2025} run the mirror-image
experiment, asking whether a model can replicate the decisions of
human judges in a factorial experiment originally conducted on U.S.
federal judges. What that line has not yet produced is the test this
experiment runs: a sealed, pre-registered evaluation in which the
model is conditioned on one decision-maker alone---biography and a
fine-tuned adapter---and asked to predict that justice's own future
votes against a like-for-like statistical prior. The slot is exactly
the one our exam fills, and the null it returns---a persona that
changes the model without improving its predictions---is the boundary
condition the prompt-era claims must now clear.

\paragraph{Simulated respondents and personas.}
The persona condition also lands inside the debate on
language-model-simulated people. \citet{argyle2023} show that
biography-conditioned models can reproduce the survey-response
distributions of specific human subpopulations---``silicon
samples''; \citet{horton2023} proposes such agents as simulated
\emph{homo silicus} for economic inquiry; \citet{dillion2023} ask
whether models can replace human research participants altogether.
Our null is a boundary condition on the optimistic reading, and we
state its scope precisely. Silicon samples are validated against
population distributions of stated attitudes; we test an individual
expert against that expert's own future record, on the hardest
decisions in the domain, under seal. At that resolution---biography
plus fine-tuning on the person's own corpus---the simulated persona
acquires surface posture (a displaced ideological dial, a
recognizable style) but not the decision function (misvotes on its
own training cases; the stylistic account of fidelity falsified).
The two results are compatible: a model can proxy the marginals of a
population's attitudes while carrying no individual decision signal
at the one-vote margin.

\paragraph{Tooling.}
QLoRA \citep{dettmers2023} makes full-model fine-tuning tractable on
free single-GPU tiers by quantizing the base model and training
low-rank adapters, which is what converts the persona question from
an institutional project into a zero-budget one. The base itself,
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct \citep{qwen25}, is deliberately small: the
design asks whether personalization adds signal, not whether scale
supplies it. Seven adapters were trained---one per justice with
training-window opinions---plus two pre-inscribed prompt-only seats
for the justices without any, each adapter sealed alongside the data
that produced it.

\paragraph{Positioning.}
Against this background, the present project narrows the claim in
four ways that we believe matter for honesty of the result. The
window is short and modern (OT2015--OT2023, an era of narrow
majorities), so historical unanimity cannot inflate accuracy. The
evaluation is pre-registered and forward-holding: a sealed set of
fifty 5--4 decisions, chosen deterministically before any model
touched them, was the only arbiter of the final conditions, and it
was scored exactly once. The bar was fixed \emph{before} the
challengers ran, at the vote level, where the attitudinal bit is
strongest---63.7\% on our window---so that any reported improvement
is an improvement over ideology itself, not over a straw man. And
when the answer came back null, the protocol did not stop at the
headline: the pre-registered probes that explain the null---the
prompt-versus-adapter decomposition, the falsified stylistic
account, and the training-case auto-coherence failure---are what
make the null informative rather than empty.
