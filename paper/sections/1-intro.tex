\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Supreme Court votes are the most consequential binary predictions in
American public life, and they are made under conditions that should
humiliate any forecaster: the inputs are thousands of pages of legal
argument, the decision rule is nine human beings, and the outcome
distribution is close enough to a coin flip that luck is difficult to
rule out. Yet a long line of work has shown that the task is not
entirely random. Statistical models built on case attributes have
predicted affirm/reverse outcomes at rates far above chance for two
decades~\citep{ruger2004,katz2017}, and the dominant theoretical
account of the Court---the attitudinal model~\citep{segal2002} ---
holds that ideology, not legal doctrine, explains most of the variance
in votes.

This paper reports the completed experiment of \emph{Legally
Subjective}, an independently conducted, zero-budget test of a
narrower and, we argue, more honest version of the prediction
question. Rather than asking how accurately a court's output can be
retrofitted by a flexible learner with centuries of data, we ask two
questions that can both fail loudly. First, is each justice's vote
predictable from the public case file alone, on a fixed, modern
window, against a pre-registered bar? Second---and this is the
experiment's live wire---if a language model is fine-tuned on one
justice's own past opinions, does it predict that justice's
\emph{future} votes better than the same model without the persona?
We built a stringent test of the second question. It did not
detectably improve prediction: on the sealed holdout of fifty hash-frozen 5--4
decisions, scored once, the persona called one case right that the
zero-shot missed and two cases wrong that the zero-shot called right
(exact McNemar, $p=1.00$), and the rest of the experiment is the
investigation of why. The two branches of the fork read differently
now that it is resolved. Had the persona helped, a
\emph{justice-specific textual signal} would have existed: the
written voice would carry predictive information about future
decisions that ideology alone does not encode (whether that signal
would be personality, drafting style, subject-matter specialization,
or something else entirely is a distinct question the design does not
settle---persona improvement is evidence of a signal, not of its
nature). It did not help, and the null is sharper than a bare
absence: personalization \emph{changed} the model
measurably---it moved individual votes, and it made every simulated
justice vote more conservatively than the justice it
imitates---without improving prediction of the outcome by a single
case. What is predictable about a close decision, at the resolution
this design can detect, is \emph{who} the justice is; the written
voice, through a persona pipeline at this scale, adds nothing that
the ideological bit does not already carry.

Three design commitments distinguish the project from prior work.
First, everything is pre-registered before the models see the
\emph{sealed} holdout: the baselines, the split, the metrics, and the
fifty-case test set are frozen in the repository, with the selection
seed and the SHA-256 of the sealed list published in advance. There is
no second holdout and no post hoc tuning. Second, the experiment is
built to be audited by a stranger: the corpus is assembled from public
sources with every raw file hash-chained; every CPU-side number in this
paper regenerates from scripts in the repository, and the GPU-dependent
stages are preserved as frozen, hash-verified artifacts. Third, the budget is
zero by construction---public data, free API tiers, and free-tier
compute. We present this strictly as a reproducibility and
accessibility constraint, not as evidence of scientific quality: it
bounds what can be tested (model scale, hyper-parameter search, number
of seeds) and it makes every result replicable by any researcher with
a laptop, but a larger budget would not weaken the design---only widen
the set of testable conditions.

The contributions of this paper are: (i) a frozen,
provenance-complete corpus of 569 argued Supreme Court cases spanning
OT2015 through OT2023, fusing CourtListener metadata with Supreme Court
Database (SCDB) vote coding, with 98.6\% oral-argument coverage for
future multimodal conditions; (ii) pre-registered statistical
baselines, of which the strongest---per-justice modal ideological
direction---sets a vote-level bar of 63.7\% on the held-out window;
(iii) a trained challenger, a family of structured-feature models whose
\emph{null result} is itself informative: nothing in the
pre-decision metadata adds stable signal beyond who the justice is
(Section~\ref{sec:protocol}); (iv) the sealed, hash-frozen exam on
fifty 5--4 decisions, executed exactly once under pre-registered
conditions---zero-shot, persona, retrieval, and the re-fit ideological
prior---whose primary outcome is a null: personalization brings no
detectable gain in case-direction prediction over the zero-shot (59.2\% against
61.2\%, $p=1.00$), while the re-fit ideological prior itself collapses
to a coin flip at case level on the same one-vote-margin decisions
(Section~\ref{sec:results}); and (v) a pre-registered mechanistic
decomposition of what personalization \emph{did} do: it displaces the
simulated ideological dial, the biographical prompt rather than the
fine-tuned adapter does the moving, a pre-registered stylistic account
of persona quality is falsified ($\rho=-0.72$), and personas misvote
on the very training cases whose texts they absorbed
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

We emphasize what this paper does not claim. It does not claim that
Supreme Court votes are unpredictable; two decades of literature and
our own baselines say otherwise. It does not treat the 63.7\% bar as
trivial: as Section~\ref{sec:protocol} shows, three families of
supervised models failed to clear it on the same rows, which is
precisely why the bar is interesting. And it does not claim that
personalization is inert: the persona condition measurably changes
vote-level behavior and the model's ideological posture---what it
never becomes is better prediction of the Court's hardest cases. The
displacement of the simulated dial is a property of the model and its
training distribution, not a causal claim about the justices
themselves.
