\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

Judge-specific personalization did not significantly improve
case-direction prediction on the sealed 5--4 test set. In the
pre-registered comparison that the seal exists to protect---persona
against zero-shot, condition B against condition A, evaluated once on
the fifty hash-frozen 5--4 decisions---B calls one case right that A
misses and two cases wrong that A gets right (exact McNemar: $b=1$,
$c=2$, $p=1.00$). The point estimates sit inside each other's
intervals, 59.2\% for B against 61.2\% for A, and the sign of the
difference is, if anything, negative. We state this first and without
qualification because it is the primary outcome of the experiment; the
remainder of the section is about what that null does and does not
mean. It does not mean the two conditions are interchangeable.
Personalization changes the model, measurably and systematically: it
displaces the simulated voting distribution toward the base model's
conservative default, it does so by different amounts for different
justices, and the displacement is visible at every level of analysis
we ran beneath the case outcome. What it never becomes is better
prediction of the outcome of the Court's hardest cases. The section traces that contrast from the
primary outcome (Section~\ref{sec:primary}) through vote-level
behavior (Section~\ref{sec:votes}) to its mechanism: a conservative
default with a displaced ideological dial
(Section~\ref{sec:dialsec}), a prompt-versus-adapter decomposition
with a pre-registered counter-example
(Section~\ref{sec:promptsec}), and a falsified stylistic explanation
(Section~\ref{sec:stylesec}).

The four conditions were scored exactly once, on the sealed fifty,
after the completeness chain from the training freeze to the exam was
verified. Figure~\ref{fig:m4-primary}(a) and
Table~\ref{tab:m4-primary} give the outcome. The three language
conditions share one open-weight base model
(Qwen/Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct); B adds, per seat, a biographical system
prompt and a QLoRA adapter fine-tuned on that justice's
training-window opinions; C adds retrieval over earlier opinions; D is
the re-fit per-justice ideological prior of
Section~\ref{sec:protocol}, included as the like-for-like statistical
comparator. Two seats have no adapter by construction---Barrett and
Jackson joined the Court after the training window closed and authored
no training-window opinions---so their condition B degenerates, by
pre-inscription, to the biographical prompt alone. On the primary
endpoint, case-direction accuracy, A scores 61.2\% (Wilson 95\% CI
[47.3; 73.6]), B 59.2\% [45.3; 71.8], C 53.1\% [39.4; 66.3], and D
50.0\% [36.4; 63.6]. With three discordant cases out of 49, only a
large true effect would have been detectable; the pre-registered test
returns $p=1.00$, and the honest summary is that personalization
brings no detectable gain on the sealed 5--4 outcomes, with a point
estimate two points below the zero-shot baseline.

Two details sharpen the null. First, the 49 scored cases of the
sealed exam split into 29
\emph{interleaved} cases---5--4 decisions handed down inside the
training era, contemporaneous with (but excluded from, by the seal)
the persona training texts---and 20 \emph{future} cases decided after
the training window closed. On the interleaved stratum, A and B
produce identical case-direction calls on every single case ($b=0$,
$c=0$); all three disagreements fall in the future stratum. The one
place the persona could plausibly have helped---cases decided after
the texts it was trained on---is exactly where its few disagreements
with A concentrate, to no net effect. Second, the re-fit ideological
prior D scores exactly 50.0\%: a coin flip, with agreement against the
always-conservative majority class of $\kappa=-0.11$. The 5--4
population removes precisely the margin that makes ideology
informative at the case level, while leaving it intact at the vote
level (75.9\%, Section~\ref{sec:votes}). The language conditions sit
between these poles, and none of them is doing much beyond the
majority class: case-direction kappa against always-conservative is
0.09 for A, 0.02 for B, 0.06 for C. On one-vote-margin decisions,
every condition---language model and statistical rule alike---is a
small perturbation of ``always guess conservative''.

The contrast with the transparent window completes the statistical
picture. Phase T---the same machinery scored on the 211
evaluation-window casefiles with every sealed case excluded, 205 of
them carrying a coded direction---had given B a $+4.9$-point advantage
over A at case level (55.1\% vs.\ 50.2\%; McNemar $b=42$, $c=32$,
$p=0.30$, not significant). Between the transparent window and the
sealed exam, the sign of the B$-$A difference flips while remaining
statistically indistinguishable from zero in both places. The honest
reading is that case-level deltas of this magnitude are noise-dominated
at these sample sizes; the sealed exam, evaluated once under a
pre-registered test, is the number that counts, and it is null.

\subsection{Vote-level behavior: changed votes, unchanged
outcomes}\label{sec:votes}

Case-direction prediction and vote prediction are different tasks. The
first asks which side wins a one-vote-margin case; the second asks how
each of the nine justices individually votes. A model can move many
individual votes without moving a single outcome---and that, it turns
out, is what personalization does. On the sealed exam's 361
current-court votes, B scores 59.8\% [54.7; 64.8] against A's 56.2\%
[51.1; 61.3], and the secondary, vote-level McNemar test is nominally
significant ($b=25$, $c=12$, $p=.047$). We report this with its full
fragility on display: it is a secondary endpoint; it has been
subjected to no multiplicity correction; and with 37 discordant votes
the $p$-value sits one tail-flip from convention. We read it as
evidence of behavioral change to be investigated---which is what the
rest of this section does---not as a confirmatory claim of
improvement. The absence of a case-level gain does not mean the
absence of a vote-level effect.

The ideological prior D dominates every language condition at the
vote level: 75.9\% [71.6; 79.8] on the 411 sealed votes cast by the
ten justices with a training-window voting record (the three departed
members included; Barrett and Jackson are necessarily outside D's
coverage, which cuts against D rather than for it). On the seven
current seats that both D and B cover, D's per-justice accuracies run
from 0.63 (Roberts) to 0.86 (Sotomayor); B's run from 0.20 (Kagan) to
0.84 (Thomas). D thus supplies the mirror image of its case-level
failure: it wins three individual votes in four and precisely cannot
predict the one-vote majority those votes aggregate into, while the
language conditions guess case direction at 53--61\% on top of
per-justice votes that are barely better than chance. The trivial
statistic captures the block structure of the Court on close cases;
the language layer does not.

Table~\ref{tab:votes-per-justice} shows where B's vote-level movement
comes from, and the pattern is the first appearance of the mechanism
the rest of this section diagnoses. B's largest gains are conservative
seats---Thomas and Alito at $+10.2$ points each, Barrett at $+10.0$
(without an adapter), Gorsuch at $+6.3$---plus Jackson ($+11.1$, no
adapter, $n=9$) and a modest $+4.1$ for Sotomayor. The two seats where
B \emph{loses} ground are Kagan ($-6.1$) and Roberts ($-6.1$). The
adapter helps where the seat already aligns with the base model's
default behavior and hurts where it does not; a model fine-tuned on a
justice's own prose ends up most accurate for the justices whose
recorded votes sit closest to that default. On the transparent window the same contrast is
larger: B beats A by 5.8 points over 1{,}713 votes (53.8\% vs.\
48.0\%; McNemar $b=364$, $c=265$, $p=9\times10^{-5}$), with B's
accuracy on the two liberal seats at 0.45 (Sotomayor) and 0.33 (Kagan)
against D's 0.67 and 0.66, while the five conservative seats never
differ by more than 0.045 between the two (Kavanaugh; all others at or
below 0.02). The vote-level shift is consistent
across windows and evaluation regimes; what it never converts into is
case-direction improvement on the sealed set.

Calibration completes the picture, and it is poor everywhere. Expected
calibration error at case level is 0.202 for A, 0.138 for B, 0.106
for C, and 0.094 for D; at vote level, 0.226, 0.169, 0.137, and
0.094 respectively (Figure~\ref{fig:m4-primary}(c)). The language
models are confidently wrong in the tail that matters most: condition
A assigns a mean liberal probability of 0.07 to 209 votes whose
observed liberal rate is 0.40. The best-calibrated of the four
conditions is the one that does not read the case at all---the prior
rule, whose outputs barely move. Probabilistic predictions from these
pipelines should not be taken at face value for any downstream use
that consumes them as probabilities.

\subsection{The ideological dial}\label{sec:dialsec}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig6_ideological_dial.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The ideological dial: actual vs.\ simulated
liberal-vote rates.} \emph{(a)}~On the sealed exam, every seat's
persona (condition B) votes more conservatively than the justice it
imitates; filled markers denote the liberal bloc. The persona-predicted
rate never exceeds 0.44 while the actual rate spans 0.16--0.86
(Jackson: $n=9$ votes). \emph{(b)}~The same dial, decomposed on the
transparent window ($n=211$ casefiles, sealed cases excluded): the
actual record, the neutral arm N ($\equiv$ condition A scheme), the
biographical prompt alone (P), and the full persona B (E1 arm
``A'', byte-identical to condition B). The base model pins every seat
near $0.11$; the prompt moves Sotomayor to $0.50$ but barely moves
Kagan ($0.05$); the adapter's marginal pull on top of the prompt is
small everywhere (Gorsuch ends at $0.00$). Actual rates are derived from
the frozen corpus and validated exactly against the published E1
arm-N accuracies. \emph{Barrett and Jackson have no adapter (degraded,
pre-inscribed mode): their persona arm \emph{is} the biographical
prompt} (open red squares).}
\label{fig:dial}
\end{figure}

To see what personalization actually moved, we measure, for each seat,
the share of cases on which a condition casts a liberal vote---the
simulated counterpart of the justice's liberal-vote rate. This dial
requires no ground truth, which lets us read it on any window.
Figure~\ref{fig:dial}(a) shows it on the sealed exam. Every one of
the nine seats lies strictly below the diagonal: every persona,
including those imitating liberal justices, votes more conservatively
than the justice it imitates. The persona-predicted liberal rate never
exceeds 0.44 (Jackson, $n=9$), while the actual rates span 0.16
(Thomas, Alito) to 0.86 (Sotomayor). The seat-level failures of
Table~\ref{tab:votes-per-justice} are not scattered noise---they are
one systematic displacement: Kagan's persona votes liberal on 4.1\% of
her sealed cases where the justice herself votes liberal on 83.7\%;
Thomas's votes liberal on 0\% where the justice does on 16.3\%. The
mechanism is compression rather than collapse. Condition B's mean
predicted probability of a liberal vote spans only 0.16--0.39 across
seats, so the Court's real ideological span is re-anchored into a
narrow band whose upper edge stays below one half. Justices already
inside that band are ``imitated'' almost perfectly; justices above it
are structurally mis-imitated, whatever the fidelity of their
adapter's prose.

Two seats in Figure~\ref{fig:dial}(a) do move materially toward the
truth---the two without adapters. Jackson's prompt-only persona
predicts a 44.4\% liberal rate against an actual 77.8\% ($n=9$;
small), and Barrett's 15.0\% against an actual 20.0\% ($n=20$),
against Kagan's adapter-backed 4.1\%. The identity carried by the
biographical prompt moves the dial more than fine-tuning on the
justice's own texts does; this observation, visible in the sealed
numbers, is what the decomposition experiment of
Section~\ref{sec:promptsec} was designed to test.

Figure~\ref{fig:dial}(b) decomposes the dial on the transparent window
(211 casefiles, sealed cases excluded, one run, fixed seed 271828)
under three arms: N, a neutral system prompt with no justice
identity---the condition-A scheme; P, the biographical prompt alone on
the base model; and B, the biographical prompt plus the seat's
adapter---the condition-B pipeline, byte-for-byte (same prompts, same
adapters). Arm N is
seat-independent by construction, and it returns 24 liberal calls in
211 cases (0.114) for every seat: the base model, asked to vote as a
neutral legal analyst, has a strong conservative default on this
task. This is the first of the three pre-registered predictions of
the decomposition experiment, and it is confirmed. The prompt arm then
moves seats in opposite directions---Sotomayor to 0.50 and Jackson to
0.18, but Kagan only to 0.05, four of the five conservative seats down
to 0.01--0.02, and Kavanaugh not at all---while the adapter's marginal
effect on top of the prompt never exceeds a tenth of the dial (at most
$+0.10$, taking Kavanaugh to 0.21): Kagan ends at 0.02, Gorsuch at
0.00, and Sotomayor holds 0.49. The persona is not a louder version
of the justice; it is the base model's default, nudged by the
biography, with the adapter contributing only a small marginal pull.

\subsection{Prompt versus adapter---and what the adapter did not
learn}\label{sec:promptsec}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig7_prompt_vs_adapter.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{E1: causal decomposition of the dial (transparent
window, $n=211$; single run, seed 271828).} Change in predicted
liberal-vote rate per seat. \emph{(a)}~Prompt effect $\Delta(P-N)$: the
pre-registered prediction (ii) --- the biography lifts liberal seats
--- holds spectacularly for Sotomayor ($+0.39$) and modestly for
Jackson ($+0.06$), but is \emph{falsified for Kagan} ($-0.06$),
highlighted in red. \emph{(b)}~Adapter effect $\Delta(B-P)$: the
marginal effect of adding the adapter on top of the prompt is small
everywhere ($\le +0.10$) and negative for the two pre-registered
targets, confirming prediction (iii) --- the adapter erases the
prompt's gain for Kagan ($-0.028$) and Sotomayor ($-0.014$).
\emph{(c)}~Combined effect $\Delta(B-N)$; open markers mark the
prompt-only seats (Barrett, Jackson --- no adapter exists). Arms:
N~$=$ neutral base model ($\equiv$ condition A scheme; conservative
default $0.114$ for all seats --- prediction (i), confirmed), P~$=$
biographical prompt, B~$=$ persona (E1 artifact arm ``A'' $\equiv$
condition B). Identity injected in the prompt is not behavior learned
by the adapter.}
\label{fig:decomposition}
\end{figure}

The decomposition experiment (E1) ran one identical vote query per
case under the three system prompts of Figure~\ref{fig:dial}(b), on
the transparent window only, with its hypotheses committed to the
repository before execution. It is confirmatory within its own
frame---a causal decomposition on non-sealed data---and it has no
bearing on the sealed primary test, which is never recomputed. Its
three pre-registered predictions and their fates are as follows.
Prediction (i), a conservative default of the neutral arm for all
seats, is confirmed ($N=0.114$ everywhere). Prediction (ii), a
positive prompt effect $\Delta(P-N)$ for each of the three liberal
seats, holds spectacularly for Sotomayor ($+0.39$) and modestly for
Jackson ($+0.06$), and is \emph{falsified for Kagan} ($-0.06$); the
broader contrast the prediction encoded---a positive mean shift for
liberal seats ($+0.13$) against a negative one for conservative seats
($-0.08$)---is confirmed. Prediction (iii), that the adapter erases
the prompt's gain for Kagan and Sotomayor, is confirmed:
$\Delta(B-P)=-0.028$ and $-0.014$ respectively
(Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition}).

Read together, the decomposition supports one central claim: the
identity injected through the prompt is not the behavior learned by
the adapter. Where the biography is ideologically legible, the prompt
alone recovers half of the dial (Sotomayor, 0.11 $\to$ 0.50); the
adapter's marginal effect on top of the prompt is small everywhere
(at most $+0.10$, for Kavanaugh) and negative for its two
pre-registered targets. In combined terms $\Delta(B-N)$, only
Sotomayor ($+0.37$) and Kavanaugh ($+0.10$) end above the neutral
default; every other adapted seat ends at or below it. The persona
pipeline does not add the justice to the model: the closest any persona
comes to its justice's record---Sotomayor's 0.49 against an actual
0.67---owes that position to the biography, and the adapter's marginal
contribution there is negative.

One measurement caveat must be stated exactly. A pre-inscribed
fidelity regression re-scored E1's arm B against the published
phase-T condition-B per-justice accuracies---same prompts, same
adapters, deterministic scoring, so exact equality was expected.
Five of the seven adapter-backed seats match exactly; Kagan (0.343
vs.\ 0.333) and Kavanaugh (0.534 vs.\ 0.554) diverge slightly, as do
the two prompt-only seats (0.560 vs.\ 0.565; 0.400 vs.\ 0.440). For
the neutral arm---seat-independent by construction, one vote query
per case replicated to the nine seats exactly as the frozen
condition-A scheme prescribes---the pre-inscribed check was a
single-seat spot-check (Roberts, the first seat of the frozen bench
ordering): 0.544 vs.\ 0.515. Extending that comparison to all nine
seats was not part of the pre-inscribed regression; it is a post-hoc
recomputation from the two published artifacts, and it widens the
picture considerably. The neutral arm's per-justice gaps run from
$-8.8$ points (Sotomayor, 0.319 vs.\ 0.407) to $+10.0$ points
(Alito, 0.625 vs.\ 0.525) with a mean absolute gap of 5.8 points,
against an aggregate shift of only $+1.5$ points (848 vs.\ 823 of
1{,}713 votes); the recorded persona-arm comparisons never diverge
by more than four votes on any seat. Inter-run divergences thus
appear between logically identical re-executions---small on the
persona arms, considerably larger and unevenly distributed across
seats on the seat-independent neutral arm---and their cause is
unisolated: consistent with, but not demonstrated to be, cuBLAS
non-determinism on the T4 GPU. The divergences themselves are a
solid observation; their cause remains a diagnostic hypothesis, not
an experimental conclusion, and pinning it down would require a
dedicated experiment (identical inputs, repeated runs, one
environment) that we have not run; nor do we have an explanation
for why the neutral arm diverges more than the persona arms.
Accordingly, every cross-arm comparison in this subsection uses
E1's internal numbers from its single seeded run, and phase-T
values are quoted only as published figures, never mixed into the
same computation.

If the adapter does not transfer the justice's decision function, it
should fail \emph{most transparently} on the cases whose texts it was
trained on. The auto-coherence probe (E3, pre-specified and
exploratory) tests this directly: it takes the training-era cases
where the justice both voted liberal \emph{and} authored the lead
opinion whose text entered the adapter's training set, and asks the
persona to vote on them. By construction, the actual liberal rate on
these cases is 1.0. Kagan's persona, trained on 21 such opinions,
votes liberal on 3 of them (14\%); no persona reaches even two-thirds
of its own probe set (the maxima are Kavanaugh's 60\% on five cases and
Sotomayor's 47\% on seventeen; Thomas's and Gorsuch's personas vote
liberal on none) (Figure~\ref{fig:e3}). The split by the judge's actual vote is more
damning than the level: for Kagan, the persona votes liberal more
often on cases where she voted \emph{conservative} (21\%) than on
cases where she voted \emph{liberal} (12\%), and the same inversion
affects Sotomayor, Thomas, and Gorsuch---four of the seven adapters.
The adapter has read the judge's words on these very cases; it did
not learn the vote that produced them.

We delimit what these two experiments establish. They are evidence
about this pipeline: one open-weight 3B base model, seven QLoRA
adapters trained on sealed-excluded opinion corpora, biographical
prompts of one fixed form, one task phrasing. Kagan's seat falsifies
the generalization ``biographical prompts lift liberal justices'';
Sotomayor's seat confirms it; the heterogeneity between them---not
either seat alone---is the finding. Nothing here licenses a claim
about LLM personas at large, and the Kagan observation in particular
is a single-seat result on 21 cases. What the two experiments do
establish, for this pipeline, is a mechanism: the persona's simulated
voting behavior is governed by the base model's default plus whatever
identity the prompt injects, and the adapter's contribution on top of
the prompt is, if anything, a pull back toward that default.

\subsection{Stylistic distinctiveness: a pre-registered prediction,
falsified}\label{sec:stylesec}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig8_e2_distinctiveness.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{E2: stylistic distinctiveness does not predict persona
fidelity --- a pre-registered prediction, falsified ($n=7$
adapters).} \emph{(a)}~Composite stylistic distinctiveness (lexical
richness, sentence-length variance, function-word and
authorial-marker signatures, TF-IDF/JSD distance to the other judges)
against persona vote fidelity on the sealed exam. The pre-registered
prediction was a \emph{positive} correlation; the observed Spearman
correlation is $\rho=-0.72$ in phase S ($-0.61$ in phase T). Kagan is
the most distinctive writer and the worst persona; Thomas the least
distinctive and among the best. \emph{(b)}~Training volume explains
nothing ($\rho=+0.14$). The surface style transfers; the decision
function does not: persona fidelity tracks the justice's alignment
with the base model's conservative default, not the distinctiveness of
the pen. Exploratory label (measures chosen after observing
fidelities; the correlation predictions themselves were inscribed in
the freeze document before execution---with the provenance caveat
stated in the text: plan and results share one commit).}
\label{fig:e2}
\end{figure}

The natural explanation left standing after E1 and E3 is stylistic:
perhaps persona fidelity tracks how distinctive a writer the justice
is, in the stylometric sense of a persistent, hard-to-fake
signature~\citep{mosteller1964,juola2006}. This explanation was itself
pre-registered---as two predictions inscribed before any
measurement---and then falsified. The distinctiveness study (E2,
local and corpus-side) scores each of the seven adapted judges on a
composite of surface measures over the clean pre-guard corpus:
sliding-window lexical richness, sentence-length variance, a
function-word signature, authorial markers such as ``I dissent'' per
ten thousand words, and TF-IDF/Jensen--Shannon distance to the other
judges (18--70 texts per judge, 76k--276k words each). The first
inscribed prediction fixed an expected ranking, with Kagan the least
distinctive writer; the observed ranking inverts it at both ends---Kagan is
the \emph{most} distinctive writer in the corpus ($+0.24$ composite
$z$) and Thomas the least ($-0.32$). The second inscribed prediction
was a positive correlation between distinctiveness and persona vote
fidelity, exceeding the volume--fidelity correlation. The observed
Spearman correlation is $\rho=-0.72$ on the sealed exam and $-0.61$
on the transparent window, against $+0.14$ for training volume
(Figure~\ref{fig:e2}): distinctiveness of the pen predicts persona
fidelity with the wrong sign.

The label discipline here matters and we keep it explicit. $\rho
=-0.72$ is the falsification of a prediction inscribed in advance,
not a hypothesis invented after seeing the results: both correlation
predictions and the full expected ranking were written into the
freeze document before the distinctiveness measures were computed,
and both were falsified---which is itself evidence against post-hoc
construction, since a prediction invented after the fact would
predict the observed sign. We state the provenance precisely:
the E2 predictions share a single freeze commit with the E2 results
(a local, CPU-side computation), so the git record alone cannot
establish their ordering; it rests on the authors' contemporaneous
records. For the kernel-executed probes E1 and E3, the git record
does establish the ordering: the plan was committed under the
\texttt{a1-freeze} tag, the pinned head is cited by the running
kernel's manifest, and the results were committed three hours later.
The distinctiveness \emph{measures}
were chosen after the fidelities had been observed, and for that
reason the study carries an exploratory label on its instruments.
One axis of Kagan's composite rests on a small sample (the
Jensen--Shannon distance over her separate opinions uses two texts),
but the negative correlation survives on the all-texts axis alone,
and no surface measure we computed correlates positively with
fidelity.

The falsification, read in the direction the data point, sharpens the
mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:dialsec}. The seven judges' surface
signatures sit at tiny Jensen--Shannon distances from one another
(0.155--0.212: American legal English is a shared register), and the
judges closest to the shared register obtain the best personas. The
persona failure is therefore not a failure of style transfer---the
style visibly moves---but of decision transfer: what varies between
justices, and what the adapter does not carry, is the voting function,
not the voice. Persona fidelity tracks the justice's alignment with
the base model's conservative default, not the distinctiveness of the
pen. A companion descriptive statistic from the phase-0 analysis
points the same way (share of majority-authored opinions against
fidelity: $\rho=-0.74$, $n=7$, no inferential status): majority
writing is coalition writing, and coalition prose is the least
individual prose a justice produces. The adapter moves words, not
votes.
