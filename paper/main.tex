\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}
We ask how predictable U.S. Supreme Court decisions are from public
information, and whether modeling individual justices improves that
predictability. We assemble a frozen, hash-chained corpus of 569 argued
cases (OT2015--OT2023, 13 justices, 4{,}569 coded votes) and fix the
price of admission with pre-registered baselines: a per-justice
ideological rule that reaches 63.7\% of votes on the held-out window,
a bar that three families of structured-feature challengers fail to
clear. The pre-registered test of the written record is a sealed exam
on fifty hash-frozen 5--4 decisions---zero-shot, persona
(biographical prompt plus a QLoRA adapter fine-tuned on each justice's
past opinions), retrieval, and the re-fit ideological prior---with
predictions frozen before any answer key existed and scored exactly
once. Personalization does not detectably improve prediction of the
Court's hardest cases: on the primary endpoint, case-direction
accuracy, the persona scores 59.18\% against the zero-shot's 61.22\%
(exact McNemar, $p=1.00$). Even the ideological prior collapses to a
coin flip at case level on these one-vote-margin decisions (50.0\%),
while at vote level it still dominates every language condition
(75.9\% against 50--60\%). Personalization does, however, change the model: it moves
individual votes (nominally, $p=.047$, unadjusted) without improving
case-direction prediction; every simulated justice votes more
conservatively than the justice it imitates; the biographical prompt,
not the fine-tuned adapter, does most of the moving; a pre-registered
stylistic account of persona quality is falsified ($\rho=-0.72$); and
personas misvote on the very training cases whose texts they absorbed.
Taken together, these results provide no evidence that modeling how
an individual justice writes adds detectable predictive power for
close case outcomes beyond the information captured by the
ideological baseline. All code, frozen artifacts, and sealed
predictions are public.
